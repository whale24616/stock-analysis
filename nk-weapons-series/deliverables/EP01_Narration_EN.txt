It is midday on October 29, 2026, off the coast of Wonsan, Gangwon Province.
Pillars of fire rise all at once from large launch vehicles lined up along the shore.
This firing took place at North Korea's first-ever drone exhibition, with Kim Jong Un watching.
North Korea called the weapon "Sniper" and said it was the first combat firing ever shown in front of a crowd.
The same day, South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff said the North had fired about ten short-range ballistic missiles that flew roughly 250 kilometers.
North Korea calls it a rocket launcher, and the South Korean military calls it a ballistic missile.
Today, let's make it simple: why one weapon has two names, how it flies, what is new, and how it can be stopped.

First, what is a multiple rocket launcher?
It is a weapon that mounts several tubes on a truck and fires rockets all at once.
Originally it had no guidance and was meant to blanket a wide area in one go.
That is why each rocket is usually only about 10 to 30 centimeters wide.
A ballistic missile is different.
It is one large round with a guidance system that sends it far and accurately.
The 600-millimeter launcher mixes the two.
At 60 centimeters across, it is several times thicker than a typical rocket launcher round.
Analysts estimate it is more than eight meters long.
Judged by the size of a single round, it is similar to a short-range ballistic missile.
But carrying several rounds on one truck and firing them in succession is like a rocket launcher.
So North Korea calls it a super-large rocket launcher, while U.S. Forces Korea named it KN-25 and classifies it as a short-range ballistic missile.
The U.S. North Korea analysis site 38 North described it as a long-range guided rocket that sits between a rocket launcher and a ballistic missile.

Now, how does it fly?
A typical ballistic missile climbs high and falls along a parabola.
Radar watches that parabola and calculates in advance where it will land.
But analysts say the 600-millimeter rocket flies much lower and flatter.
One European nonproliferation research report called its trajectory unusually flat.
According to 2020 reporting, North Korea described this kind of flight as a "low-altitude gliding and leaping flight trajectory."
It is understood to mean flying low, then rising slightly again on the way down to change its path.
Why is flying low an advantage?
Because the Earth is round, ground-based radar sees objects flying low far away later.
Seen later means less time to prepare a defense.
And if the path changes in flight, the planned intercept point has to be recalculated.
The secret to changing its path lies in the fins.
Researchers who analyzed public photos see four small control fins at the front and four fixed fins at the back.
The front fins are thought to move to correct its attitude and direction in flight.
In other words, it is not just a rocket that is thrown, but one that corrects its path as it flies.

So what changed in 2026?
On January 27, North Korea said it test-fired an upgraded large-caliber rocket launcher with new technology.
The North claimed four rounds hit a sea target 358.5 kilometers away, and the Joint Chiefs said they flew about 350 kilometers.
The notable change was the launch vehicle.
Analysts said the tubes increased from four to five, and the devices that raise the tubes went from two to one.
Experts saw it as fixing the problems of rapid follow-up fire and mobility exposed in the Russia-Ukraine war.
It means firing more from one vehicle and leaving the spot faster.
Kim Jong Un named an "autonomous precision-guided flight system" as the weapon's key feature.
It is a claim that the weapon can ignore any outside interference.
It reads as meaning the rocket finds its own way even if satellite navigation signals are jammed.
Some experts have raised the possibility that Russia handed over military satellite navigation technology, but this is not a confirmed fact.
On February 18, a ceremony in Pyongyang handed 50 of these new launch vehicles over to the military.
North Korea claimed it had built 50 more in just two months.
At a March 14 drill, 12 vehicles each fired one round, and Kim directly mentioned enemies within a 420-kilometer range.
Then on October 29, this launcher shared the stage with drones.
Analysts say the North wanted to show a pairing in which drones find targets and the launcher strikes them.
Kim named making unmanned weapons artificially intelligent as the top priority.
On the number fired, the Joint Chiefs said about ten, while one South Korean military expert analyzed photos and concluded fifteen flew at once.

Let's sum up these features from the defender's point of view.
The first is distance.
Draw a 420-kilometer circle from near the DMZ, and it reaches the southern tip of the peninsula.
The second is numbers.
When many rounds come at once, the defender must fire just as many interceptors at once.
The third is a low, changing trajectory.
The fourth is mobility.
If a truck hides, comes out, fires and leaves right away, it is hard to find before it shoots.
The fifth is that it is hard to know what is coming.
North Korea has claimed this launcher can carry the Hwasan-31 tactical nuclear warhead.
Not knowing whether an incoming round is conventional or nuclear makes the decision even harder.

So how is South Korea preparing?
South Korea's response can be divided into three broad stages.
The first stage is finding it before it fires.
This is called Kill Chain.
In November 2025, South Korea launched its fifth military reconnaissance satellite, completing a five-satellite system.
The Defense Ministry said this completed the reconnaissance foundation of Kill Chain.
The goal is to use satellites and drones to find where launch vehicles hide and stop them before they fire.
The second stage is hitting incoming rounds and bringing them down.
Korean missile defense is built in several layers.
The lower layer is handled by Patriot and Cheongung-II.
L-SAM, the long-range surface-to-air missile for the layer above, has entered mass production, and the Defense Ministry has budgeted to bring its deployment forward.
The third stage is massive punishment and retaliation, striking back harder if attacked.
The very fact of being able to strike back acts as deterrence that keeps the other side from firing in the first place.
On top of this, important facilities are spread out and hardened.
That is because an attack that arrives in large numbers at once is hard to stop with interceptors alone.

There are, however, things to view with caution.
"Ultra-precision," "100 percent hits" and "50 more built in two months" are all North Korean claims.
None has been independently confirmed.
Nor has it been confirmed that the Hwasan-31 has actually been fitted to this launcher.
The idea that Russian technology went into it is also speculation.
The flight distance confirmed in October was about 250 kilometers, and 420 kilometers is the maximum North Korea has stated.
Hong Min, a senior research fellow at the Korea Institute for National Unification, assessed that this "Sniper" is likely an existing system rather than a completely new weapon.

Let's wrap up.
The 600-millimeter launcher is called a rocket launcher, but each round is as big as a missile and corrects its path in flight.
And its numbers are as large as a rocket launcher's.
The changes in 2026 point toward firing more, faster, and resisting interference.
For the defender, finding it before it fires, stopping it in layers and having the power to strike back are all needed together.
