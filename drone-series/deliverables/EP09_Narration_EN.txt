It is autumn 2026, on a country road in Donetsk, eastern Ukraine.
Above the rain-soaked road, netting stretches for kilometres like a tunnel.
Beneath it, a small van wrapped in wire mesh races along, throwing up mud.
On a clear day, this van could not have moved.
Because drones are watching from the sky.
So the soldiers here wait for rain and fog.
Today, let's look at how Ukrainian soldiers survive in the kill zone at the front, where drones target everything that moves.

What is the kill zone?
Britain's Financial Times reported that the area within about 15 kilometres of the front has become a kill zone.
Inside it, small FPV drones target anything that moves, people and vehicles alike.
The FPV drones we saw in Episode 3 now cover the whole front line.
How deep the kill zone reaches differs from area to area.
Some reports say it reaches 20 to 25 kilometres in some areas.
One Ukrainian corps commander expects it could widen to 30 kilometres by the end of this year.
Inside it, transport by trucks and other vehicles has almost stopped.
Soldiers move in scattered groups of two or three, and sometimes crawl for days under cloaks that hide their body heat.
These cloaks make the body harder to see on a drone's thermal camera.
But there are still few of them, and assessments say their effect depends on the situation.
Some positions reportedly take three days to reach on foot.
America's NPR reported that in some areas, there are fewer than ten soldiers per 1.6 kilometres.
Many soldiers dig deep and hold out hiding in underground positions.
Even wounded soldiers sometimes cannot get out right away, and wait in their positions for days or even weeks.
People even say that drones bring the supplies and robots bring back the wounded.
The danger is not only at the front.
In October 2025, a Russian drone attacked a pickup truck in the city of Kramatorsk, about 19 kilometres from the front.
Since then, there have also been reports of vehicles hit by drones on the road between Kramatorsk and Sloviansk.
Of course, the kill zone exists for both sides.
Because Ukraine is holding back Russian troops the same way.
With its Drone Line project, costing about 880 million dollars, Ukraine set the goal of stopping Russian troops from moving freely within 10 to 15 kilometres of the front.
So Russian troops have also switched to slipping through in twos and threes, and reports say they use bad weather, when drones see poorly.
From here, let's look at the Ukrainian shields for surviving inside it.

The first shield is netting.
Drones come down from above and crash into their target.
So if you cover a road with netting, drones get caught in it and cannot reach the vehicles.
Ukraine's State Special Transport Service, under the defence ministry, said it has put up this netting over more than 1,640 kilometres of road since the start of 2026.
About 87 kilometres were added in October alone.
The defence ministry has also set a goal of covering another 4,000 kilometres this year.
However, at the current pace, that goal is still a long way off.
Netting has weaknesses too.
It can burn or melt, and drones are reported to sometimes hide under the netting and wait.
Netting is not only on front-line roads.
In cities near the front where drones fly into the streets, netting is strung over the roads people walk on.
Skies covered in netting are no longer an unusual sight in Ukraine.
Still, every kilometre of netting means safer evacuations and faster supply.

The second shield is jamming.
Ordinary FPV drones are controlled by radio.
So blasting strong noise can cut the link between the pilot and the drone.
But Russia has increased the number of drones that avoid this weakness.
Fiber-optic drones.
They carry a fiber-optic line as thin as a hair on their tail, unspooling it for kilometres as they fly.
Because the control signals travel along the line, not by radio, jamming does not work.
Some analyses say that with longer lines they can fly tens of kilometres away.
Ukraine's commander-in-chief Syrskyi admitted that Russia is ahead in fiber-optic drones, in both numbers and range of use.
In January 2026, he also said Ukraine was still catching up in this field.
Wherever fiber-optic drones pass, kilometres of line are left across fields and roads like a spider's web.
Experts say there is still no way to stop fiber-optic drones at scale.
In the end, the only options left are to shoot them down, entangle them, or block them.
Ukraine is working to find fiber-optic drones first with sound and thermal cameras instead of radio.
Once found, they must in the end be stopped physically.

So the third shield is the defence of the last moment.
The simplest weapon is the shotgun.
Ukrainian special forces are training to shoot FPV drones with shotguns.
Shotguns are not affected by jamming, and the small pellets spread widely, so they can hit even fast drones.
But there are concerns that newer Russian drones are bigger and tougher, and ordinary shot often fails to bring them down.
So new ammunition made with heavier materials is also being tested.
To stop fiber-optic drones, a device has even appeared that spins barbed wire with a motor to wind up and cut the line.

What stands out most these days is firing nets.
Ukrainian company Phantom Defense has built a net launcher called Mangal.
When a drone approaches, it fires a weighted net six metres by six metres, which wraps the drone and brings it down.
One net shot reportedly costs around 45 dollars.
It has been used on a trial basis at the front since March 2026, has also been mounted on a ground robot, and is now heading for mass production.
The developer describes it as a second line of defence that can stop even fiber-optic drones when jamming fails.
There are also interceptor drones that fire nets in the sky.
An interceptor drone called Spider, made by WINFLY, reportedly catches enemy drones with a net, then returns undamaged and flies again.
The developer says each net shot costs about 27 dollars.
However, most of these performance and kill records are the developers' own claims and have not been independently verified.

The fourth shield is robots that move instead of people.
The fewer times people enter the kill zone, the more lives can be saved.
So Ukraine has greatly expanded unmanned ground vehicles, so-called ground robots.
According to Ukraine's defence ministry, supply and evacuation missions by ground robots rose from about 7,500 in January 2026 to more than 31,000 in October.
That is more than a fourfold increase in ten months.
More than 22,000 ground robots have been contracted this year alone.
In September, one robot crossed a road covered by mines and drones and brought back four wounded soldiers at once.
In the sky, large heavy-lift drones carry ammunition and water.
One brigade said it makes more than 1,500 supply runs a month with these large drones alone.
Of course, robots are attacked too, and many never come back.
Still, losing a robot is better than losing a soldier.

Let's sum up.
Surviving the kill zone means hiding, covering, blocking, and sending machines instead of people.
Netting over roads, jamming, shotguns and net launchers, and ground robots protect soldiers layer upon layer.
But as with Russia's fiber-optic drones, when one side builds a new shield, the other builds a spear to pierce it.
The spear and shield of drone warfare change every day, even on the dirt roads of the front.
