It is October 2026, beside a bridge in Kyiv.
A large machine gun on a mount points at the sky.
No one is turning it by hand, yet the barrel slowly turns on its own, scanning the sky.
When the radar picks up a jet drone, the barrel stops on one point and fires a short burst.
On October 5th, Ukraine's Air Force spokesman Ihnat said robotic turrets like this had begun shooting down Geran-5 jet drones.
He admitted that at first he had been a big skeptic himself.
Today, let's look at the AI machine guns guarding Kyiv, and the invisible network behind them that finds drones by sound and radio.

Why did machines become necessary?
The jet drones we saw in the last episode fly in at around 500 kilometres an hour.
That means moving almost 140 metres every second.
Finding a target like that in the night sky with human eyes, and aiming at it by hand, is very hard.
So Ukraine thinks of its defence like a human body.
First the ears that listen, then the shield that makes drones lose their way, and finally the hands that shoot.
Let's look at them one by one.

The first is the ears.
On poles across Ukraine sit small devices made of a mobile phone and a microphone.
The acoustic detection network they form together is called Sky Fortress.
In 2022, Ukrainian engineers began building it to catch Shahed drones.
Radar can easily miss small drones flying low.
But propeller drones make a distinctive sound, like a motorbike.
The microphone on the pole hears that sound, and artificial intelligence works out whether it is a drone.
When several poles hear the same sound, the difference in when it reached each one can be used to calculate the drone's position and direction.
This information is gathered centrally and sent straight to the tablet screens of fire teams in the field.
It is also how the mobile fire teams on machine-gun trucks we saw in Episode 6 knew where drones would come from in the dark.
More than 10,000 sensors are now reported to be spread across Ukraine.
The most surprising part is the price.
General Hecker, then commander of US Air Forces in Europe, said each sensor costs 400 to 500 dollars.
And he said the whole network cost less than two Patriot missiles.
He also said the network played a big role in one attack in which 80 of 84 incoming drones were shot down.
The US said it wanted to test the system itself, and a senior NATO general said the alliance should invest in such cheap acoustic sensors.
Of course, it has weaknesses too.
Strong wind or city noise can mask the sound of a drone.
And sound is slower than you might think.
By the time the sound of a fast target like a jet drone is heard, the drone is already much further ahead.
That is why the acoustic data is combined with radar data.

The second sense is radio.
To be controlled from afar or to send data, a drone has to give off radio signals.
Ukraine's military said that since November 2025, Russia has been steadily fitting Geran drones with communication devices called mesh modems.
These devices reportedly let Russian operators control the drones from hundreds of kilometres away.
But according to one Ukrainian expert, these modems have strong signals, so they are easily picked up by signals-intelligence equipment and even by small radio analysers.
The device that made the drone smarter has become a beacon that gives away its own position.
Ukrainian defence adviser Beskrestnov explained that the relay stations passing on these signals are often placed on tall towers near the border, and that Ukraine finds their locations.
Some of them were reportedly found in Belarus.

The third is the shield.
Geran drones find their way to the target by checking their position with satellite signals.
The easiest method is jamming, which covers those signals with loud noise.
But a Geran keeps flying on inertial navigation even when the satellite signal is cut.
So Ukraine uses a different method.
It sends fake satellite signals that trick the drone into thinking it is somewhere else.
It is like handing a traveller the wrong map.
Ukraine's electronic warfare system that does this is called Pokrova.
Ukraine's Air Force confirmed in 2024 that Pokrova was in real use.
France's Le Monde reported that in one attack in November 2024, 95 of 188 Russian drones lost their way because of fake signals.
It also reported that between November 24th and 26th, 43 drones flew off towards neighbouring Belarus.
However, little has been made public about how well Pokrova works, so it is hard to verify independently.
And if a drone leaves the jamming zone and picks up the real signal again, it can correct its course.
So a shield alone is not enough, and in the end you need hands.

Fourth, today's main character: the hands.
In May 2025, Ukraine unveiled an AI turret called Sky Sentinel.
The turret, fitted with a 12.7-millimetre heavy machine gun, turns a full 360 degrees.
Here is how it works.
First, radar tells it roughly where the drone is.
Then a camera finds and locks on to the target, and artificial intelligence calculates the drone's speed, direction and even the wind.
Then it aims at the spot the drone is about to pass through.
One Ukrainian military expert explained that the turret fires a short burst ahead into the drone's path, creating a cloud of fragments for the drone to fly into.
A calculation that would take a person several seconds by hand, the machine does in an instant.
Spokesman Ihnat said that because AI removes human error, it can hit even jet drones.
The software was built by Ukrainian engineers themselves, but some of the optics and rangefinders are still foreign-made.
At first it caught propeller Shaheds, and one unit commander said it downed six Shaheds in six engagements.
Then on October 4th, 2026, President Zelensky said more than 12 of these turrets had been installed in Kyiv, with 8 more to be placed around the city.
Turrets are also being set up next to important sites such as bridges.
The next day, the Air Force announced that the turrets had actually shot down jet drones.
On October 19th, President Zelensky said more capacity for turret production had been found, and that orders would be increased.
The price is worth noting too.
According to reports, one turret costs about 150,000 dollars, similar to the estimated price of a single jet Geran.
But unlike a missile, a turret does not disappear after one use.
The developers estimate that protecting one city takes about 10 to 30 of these turrets.
However, these kill records are Ukrainian announcements and have not been independently confirmed.

Of course, AI turrets are not all-powerful either.
Spokesman Ihnat said the turret's reach is just over one kilometre.
Because it is a machine gun, it cannot shoot distant drones, and it can only protect the bridge or building right next to it.
So he stressed that the turret is not a cure-all, but one layer of a multi-layered air defence.
President Zelensky also said small domestically made missiles for catching jet drones would start to be used from late October.
Fighter jets and missiles stop drones far away, interceptor drones in the middle, and turrets in the last kilometre.

Let's sum up.
Microphones on poles listen for the sound, and radio equipment picks up the drone's signals.
Fake satellite signals make the drone wander, and AI turrets hit the drones that still get close.
What this defence network has in common is that it is cheap, made by Ukraine itself, and changes quickly to keep up with an ever-faster enemy.
Guarding the sky is no longer just the work of human eyes and hands, but of machine ears and hands as well.
