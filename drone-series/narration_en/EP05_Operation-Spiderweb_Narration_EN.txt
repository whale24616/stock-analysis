It is Sunday afternoon, June 1st, 2025.
An ordinary truck pulls over by a road in Russian Siberia.
On its flatbed sits a box that looks like a small wooden house.
Then its roof slowly opens.
Dozens of small drones rise out of it.
They head straight for a nearby air base.
There, Russia's big bombers stand parked in rows.
This base is more than 4,000 kilometres from Ukraine.
At almost the same moment, the same thing happened at other Russian bases.
Ukraine called this Operation Spiderweb.
Today, using what has been made public, let's walk through how it was prepared and carried out.

After the war began, Russian bombers took off from deep inside Russia.
From far away, they fired missiles at Ukrainian cities.
Ukraine could stop some of the incoming missiles.
But it could barely touch the bombers that carried them.
The bases where they sat were simply too far away.
Some were near the Arctic, and some were in the middle of Siberia.
So Ukraine changed the question.
If you can't fly that far, take off right next door.
Ukrainian officials said the operation took a year and a half to prepare.

Think of the Trojan horse from the old story.
Soldiers hid inside a giant wooden horse and were carried into the city.
Spiderweb worked in a similar way.
A small drone can't fly even a few dozen kilometres on its own.
But a truck can drive thousands of kilometres.
The road network became the drone's fuel tank.
Here is how SBU chief Vasyl Maliuk later described the preparations.
First, they rented an office and a warehouse in the Russian city of Chelyabinsk.
It was reportedly not far from an office of Russia's security service.
The drones were hidden in boxes built to look like mobile wooden cabins.
Trucking small wooden cabins around is a common sight in Russia.
The drones sat in a compartment just under the roof.
Batteries and solar panels inside reportedly kept the drones charged.
Trucks and drivers were arranged separately.
According to reports, the drivers did not know what they were carrying.
Near the destination, each driver got a phone call telling them where to stop.
Once the truck stopped, the roof opened by remote control.
There was a moment when it almost fell apart.
On one truck, the roof reportedly came off about 290 kilometres into the trip.
One account says the driver pulled over and saw the hidden drones.
Ukraine said 117 drones were used in the operation.
Radar watches the sky.
But no one watches every cargo truck on every road.

So how do you fly a drone that is thousands of kilometres away?
Normally a drone can only go as far as its controller's radio signal reaches.
The defence analysts at Janes studied video released from the operation.
On screen was ArduPilot, a free flight program anyone can download.
This program can connect to a distant pilot over a mobile phone network.
Janes judged that the drones may have been flown from inside Ukraine over Russia's own mobile networks.
Think of a phone call instead of a walkie-talkie.
The signal goes from the drone to a nearby cell tower, across the internet, to the pilot.
But the longer the path, the later each command can arrive.
So the flight program helps the drone keep its balance and follow its route on its own.
The human only has to decide where to go and what to hit.
Here is the part worth noticing.
A truck, a wooden cabin, a phone network, free software, small drones.
None of it was a secret weapon.
What was new was the way ordinary things were tied together.

The attacks came at several bases almost at once.
Olenya in the Arctic, Belaya in Siberia, and Dyagilevo and Ivanovo closer to Moscow.
A fifth base in Russia's Far East was also targeted, but that attack reportedly failed.
Why strike them all at the same time?
Because surprise can be used only once.
The moment one base is hit, the others go on alert.
They can move planes, cover them, or stop suspicious trucks.
So every strike had to land before the alarm spread.
Remember the boats in Episode 4 that carried small drones?
This time, the truck played that role.
A big carrier covered the distance, and small drones made the final precise strike.

Ukraine claimed it hit 41 aircraft, including bombers and early-warning planes.
It estimated the damage at about 7 billion dollars.
But these figures are Ukraine's claims.
Independent assessments were lower.
The Insider, an independent Russian outlet that analysed satellite images, counted 15 aircraft confirmed destroyed.
According to Reuters, US officials assessed about 10 destroyed and up to 20 damaged.
Even the lower numbers matter.
Russia no longer builds Tu-95 or Tu-22M3 bombers.
The Tu-160 is built only in tiny numbers.
Lost bombers are not easy to replace.
A few small drones took down aircraft that are hard to replace.
But the biggest shock was a change in thinking.
For a long time, armies divided the world into a dangerous front and a safe rear.
Spiderweb showed that for aircraft parked on the ground, there may be no safe rear.

That shock has carried into 2026.
The Insider reported that Russia moved many of its remaining bombers to more distant bases in the Far East.
Still, the attacks continued.
In July and August 2026, Tu-95 bombers at Engels air base were hit one after another.
The SBU said the August strike used long-range drones that flew about 800 kilometres, not trucks.
Reports said satellite images showed a bomber with a badly broken wing.
Russia is building large roofed shelters for its bombers at Engels.
In June 2026 satellite images, at least 17 appeared to be under construction.

Of course, operations like this have limits.
Spiderweb depended almost entirely on surprise.
Once the world has seen it, the same surprise is hard to pull off again.
Since May 2025, Russia has repeatedly cut mobile internet in many regions.
The Russian government says this is to keep drones from using phone networks.
The surest defence is old and simple.
Put a strong roof over the aircraft that small drones can't get through.
Cheaper options are covering planes with nets or spreading them across many bases.
Now the price of a concrete shelter is being weighed against one truck full of cheap drones.

Spiderweb showed what is possible when a big carrier brings small drones close.
But Ukraine is not the only side attacking with drones.
Russia also sends hundreds of drones into Ukraine every night.
They are Geran drones, an Iranian design that Russia now mass-produces.
