It is October 2026, in the sky over Kyiv, Ukraine's capital.
A fast-moving dot appears on the radar.
It is a Russian jet drone flying at over 400 kilometres an hour.
A small Ukrainian drone rises up to block its path.
Then a small flash bursts in the sky.
Ukraine's former defence minister Fedorov said a new interceptor drone had shot down two of these jet drones within a few days.
Today, let's look at the biggest problem for Ukraine's air defence in 2026: jet drones, and the speed race to catch them.

In the last episode, we saw Geran drones that fly on a propeller.
Their speed is about 185 kilometres an hour.
That is why machine-gun trucks and propeller interceptor drones could catch them.
But Russia began fitting its drones with jet engines.
Ukraine's military intelligence says the newest Geran-5 flies at 450 to 600 kilometres an hour.
That is nearly three times faster than the old Geran.
The shape changed too.
Instead of a triangular wing, it has a long body with straight wings, and looks like a cruise missile.
Its engine is reported to be a small Chinese-made jet engine.
And the numbers grew fast.
Reports say jet-drone attacks rose from about 350 in June 2026 to nearly 2,800 in August.
That is an eightfold jump in just three summer months.

Why is a faster drone harder to stop?
Let's do the simple math.
Say there are 10 kilometres left between where the drone is spotted and the city.
At 185 kilometres an hour, it takes more than three minutes to arrive.
At 500 kilometres an hour, it takes just over a minute.
The time to stop it shrinks to nearly a third.
The same principle we saw with Ukraine's jet missile-drones in Episode 2 is now aimed back at Ukraine.
And time is not the only problem.
Propeller interceptor drones often chased their targets from behind and rammed them.
But if the target is much faster than you, you can never catch it from behind.
You have to wait across its path, or build a faster hunter.
On top of that, jet drones sometimes fly above 3 kilometres, where machine guns can barely reach.

The result shows in the numbers.
Ukrainian officials said 2,100 jet drones flew in during just the first three weeks of September.
Since the Geran-5 first appeared in January, it has often been fast enough to outrun Ukrainian interceptor drones.
So Ukraine went looking for a new kind of hunter to catch jet drones.
Most of the interceptor drones that had protected Ukraine until then were built to catch slow propeller drones.
Jet drones needed a completely new hunter.

Brave1, Ukraine's defence technology agency, began funding counters to jet drones in the summer of 2025.
It says 22 teams are now building fast interceptor drones, and 12 of them receive government funding.
Seven types of interceptors have already been used in combat.
In September, it was also announced that 67 companies were working on interceptors for jet drones.
The designs vary.
Some use small jet engines, some spin propellers with powerful electric motors, and some push air with a fan inside a duct.
In late September, a Ukrainian unit reportedly used a new interceptor to down a Geran-5 flying at 425 kilometres an hour, more than 3 kilometres up.
It was that interceptor's first success.

Another idea is to turn an attack weapon around.
Ukraine has a jet drone called Bars, originally built to strike Russia.
Ukraine converted Bars into an interceptor that hunts drones in the sky.
In early October, President Zelensky said Bars had shown about a 30 percent hit rate against jet drones, the best result so far.
Thirty percent may sound low, but considering it means hitting a target flying at hundreds of kilometres an hour in mid-air, it is far from low.
There were also reports that Germany agreed to fund the joint production of 2,000 Bars drones.

Then, on October 12th, came the scene from the start.
Fedorov said a new interceptor built with the special forces unit Lasar's Group had shot down two Geran-5s heading for Kyiv.
He said it took only one month to go from idea to real kills.
A human flies it for now, but the goal is to make it find and catch targets on its own with artificial intelligence.
He also announced plans to build hundreds of them a month by the end of 2026.
However, most of these kill records are Ukrainian announcements and have not been independently confirmed.

There is a big obstacle in this race.
It is the engine.
Fast interceptor drones need small, powerful jet engines.
But President Zelensky said Ukraine relies mostly on imports for these engines, and domestic production is still too small.
Government orders have already passed 1,000 interceptors, but production is still ramping up.
Even the earlier propeller interceptors took about half a year to reach mass production.
In the meantime, fighter jets are helping too.
President Zelensky said Ukraine will receive new 20-millimetre cannon rounds for its F-16s from allies to raise the jet-drone kill rate.

Russia is not standing still either.
One Russian military expert claimed the Geran-5 reached 800 kilometres an hour, but this has not been confirmed.
When one side gets faster, the other gets faster still, and then the first side speeds up again.
The cycle of attack and counter we saw in Episode 3 has now become a speed race in the sky.

Catching jet drones is, in the end, a fight over speed and time.
The side that builds more, faster hunters, more quickly, holds the sky.
But as speeds outrun human eyes and hands, defences where machines aim and fire on their own have appeared.
