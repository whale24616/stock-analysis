This time, it's a small drone that flies only a few kilometres.
A drone about the size of two palms races low and fast across a field.
The pilot sits in a trench a few kilometres away, wearing goggles.
Whatever the drone's camera sees, the pilot sees too.
Ten years ago, machines like this were hobby racers zipping around gym halls.
Today, they're one of the most common weapons on Ukraine's front line.
One costs about 400 dollars, roughly the price of a smartphone.
And the newest ones unspool a hair-thin glass thread behind them as they fly.
It's a link that radio jamming can't cut.
Today, let's break down, simply, how FPV drones work and why they changed the face of war.

In the first year of the war, small drones were mostly eyes.
Store-bought camera drones found the enemy and told the artillery where to fire.
But both sides began running short of shells.
So soldiers asked a simple question.
What if the drone itself were the weapon?
The answer came from an unexpected place: hobby drone racing.
Racers wear goggles and fly small, nimble drones as if they were sitting inside them.
FPV stands for first-person view, seeing through your own eyes.
Through 2023 and 2024, FPV drones turned the area around the front into a space that is watched all the time.
Soldiers call it the kill zone.
In May 2026, the commander of Ukraine's drone forces said this kill zone had grown to more than 25 kilometres on each side of the front.
To understand how that happened, we first need to look inside the drone.

An FPV drone's skeleton is almost the same as a racing drone's.
Four arms, four propellers and one battery, and that's nearly it.
The body is usually made of carbon fibre.
It's light, stiff and cheap to make.
A stiff body shakes less, and less shaking keeps the sensors from getting confused.
At the end of each arm, a small electric motor spins tens of thousands of times a minute.
Each motor has a small controller that feeds it power in exactly the right order.
It's like pushing a swing at just the right moment, thousands of times a second.
The brain is a small circuit board called the flight controller.
It holds sensors that feel tilting and shaking.
A drone lifted by four propellers is naturally hard to balance.
So the flight controller keeps it level by adjusting all four motors hundreds of times a second.
The pilot chooses the direction, and the computer does the balancing.
Next come the radio links that act as eyes and ears.
Video from a tiny camera travels by radio to the pilot's goggles.
In the other direction, the pilot's finger movements travel to the drone on another radio signal.
Two radio links, running in opposite directions.
As we'll see, these two links are exactly the FPV drone's weak point.

So why did hobby parts beat purpose-built military gear?
The answer is the supply chain.
Motors, cameras, batteries and circuit boards are made in enormous numbers worldwide, and many of them in China.
That makes them cheap, standard and easy to swap.
Ukraine's early production started in garages and apartments.
Volunteers bought parts with crowdfunded money and assembled the drones themselves.
Over time, the government set up purchasing schemes and an online marketplace for units, and a domestic parts industry grew.
The numbers climbed fast.
Ukraine's Defence Ministry said it supplied about three million FPV drones to the military in 2025.
That's roughly two and a half times the year before.
Russia ramped up production in much the same way.
But a drone is only as good as its pilot.
Flying FPV is hard.
You have to handle speed, angle and height all at once.
So training usually starts on a computer simulator.
Cheap parts, mass production, and thousands of skilled pilots.
That's the FPV formula.
But the formula had one big weakness.
Radio.

Remember those two radio links from earlier?
Radio jamming goes after exactly these.
A jammer doesn't need to be clever.
It just has to shout louder than the pilot on the channel the drone is listening to.
Near the target especially, the pilot's signal is at its weakest and the jammer's at its strongest.
The goggle screen dissolves into snow, and the drone goes astray.
Analysts believe a large share of FPV drones have been stopped this way.
The first answer was surprisingly simple.
Don't use radio at all.
A fibre-optic drone carries a spool of glass thread as thin as a hair and pays it out behind as it flies.
Video and commands travel through that thread as light.
Light inside a thread can't be jammed by radio.
And because it sends no radio signal, it's also hard to work out where the pilot is.
Russia used them in combat first, in Kursk in August 2024, and Ukraine soon followed.
But the thread is also a weakness.
The spool is heavy, cutting flight time or the load it can carry.
The thread can snag on trees or power lines.
And the drone can only fly as far as its thread is long.
Early on that was about ten kilometres, but 2026 reports say they now reach 30 to 40.
The second answer is software: AI terminal guidance.
The pilot picks a target on the screen.
If the radio drops out at the last moment, the drone's computer keeps following that spot on the screen and finishes the remaining distance by itself.
It isn't a robot that decides on its own what to attack.
That decision is still made by a person.
The machine just holds on after the link is lost.

So how much has this little drone changed the battlefield?
Ukraine claims that in 2026, most Russian losses, as much as 90 percent or more, are caused by drones.
However, that figure hasn't been independently verified, and one independent Ukrainian analyst puts it at about 70 to 80 percent.
The arithmetic is brutal.
A drone costing a few hundred dollars can stop an armoured vehicle worth millions.
In July 2026, Ukraine said a roughly 400-dollar FPV drone had hit a Russian attack helicopter.
The drone's maker puts the helicopter's value at about 19 million dollars.
This claim, too, hasn't been independently confirmed.
So near the front, troops now move only at night, in bad weather, or in very small groups.
Anything seen gets spotted, and anything spotted can be hit within minutes.
At the other end of the small-drone scale are big bomber drones.
These six- or eight-rotor drones are called Baba Yaga by Russian soldiers, after a witch from Slavic folk tales.
They mostly fly at night with thermal cameras, carry far heavier loads than an FPV, and come back to be used again.
Some also drop supplies to cut-off positions.

That doesn't mean FPV drones can't be stopped.
The first defences were physical.
Troops weld wire cages over vehicles and string nets over roads to catch drones before they arrive.
Some supply routes are reportedly covered by net tunnels for kilometres.
Jamming still works well against radio-linked drones.
Now small jammers are fitted to vehicles, and soldiers even carry them.
At close range, more and more soldiers carry shotguns.
It's an old answer to small, fast-flying targets.
And both sides are building interceptor drones.
Fast drones that hunt other drones in the air.
Every counter brings another counter.
Jamming led to fibre optics, fibre optics led to nets, and nets lead to the next idea.
This cycle turns in months, not years.

FPV drones show how a hobby can become a huge industry in wartime.
And they show how quickly countermeasures follow.
