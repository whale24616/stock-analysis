To understand why these drones look the way they do, we first need to understand the designers' dilemma.

When you design a long-range drone, you have to get three things right at once.
Fly far, carry enough, and cost little.
The problem is, improve one, and the other two get worse.
So designers use a few tricks.
Trick number one is the wing.
This drone's wings are long and slender.
A lot like a glider's.
A wing lifts by pushing air downward, but at the wingtips the air curls up and around into a vortex.
That vortex becomes drag, pulling the aircraft back.
The longer and thinner the wing, the weaker the vortex, and the more fuel you save.
Trick number two is putting the propeller at the back.
Like an outboard motor pushing a boat, it pushes the aircraft from behind.
That frees up the nose for payload and equipment, and the wing gets clean, undisturbed air, which makes it more efficient.
Trick number three is materials.
Instead of expensive aerospace metal, they use plastic composites, sponge-like foam and simple tubing.
They're cheap, light and fast to build.
Less metal also means a weaker radar echo.
But the engine and fuel tank are still metal, so these drones aren't truly stealthy.
Finally, the heaviest items, fuel and payload, sit in the middle of the aircraft, close to the centre of gravity.
That way, the balance holds even as the fuel burns off.

So why use a small piston engine, like a motorbike's, instead of a jet engine?
These drones fly slowly, at around 150 to 200 kilometres per hour.
At that speed, a small engine turning a propeller burns far less fuel than a jet.
Jet engines are efficient when flying fast, but at low speed they guzzle fuel.
How far an aircraft can fly comes down to three main things.
How efficiently the engine uses fuel, how little drag the aircraft has, and how much of its total weight is fuel.
But there's a catch.
The more fuel you add, the less extra range each kilo buys.
Because the fuel itself is heavy.
And every extra kilogram of fuel is a kilogram of payload you can't carry.
So some drones carry a small load very far, and others carry a bigger load a little less far.
Reported figures suggest tens of kilograms of payload, and ranges from several hundred to over fifteen hundred kilometres.
Flying a thousand kilometres takes five to seven hours.
So during all that time, how does the drone find its way?

Surprisingly, for most of the flight, no one is steering these drones.
Radio signals travel in straight lines, but the Earth is round.
A low-flying drone drops below the horizon after just a few dozen kilometres, and the signal can't reach it.
So the drone has to find its own way.
Method one is inertial navigation.
It's like walking with your eyes closed, counting how many steps you take in which direction.
Sensors inside the drone keep calculating its movement to estimate where it is.
It needs no outside signal, so it can't be jammed.
But small errors add up, and over time the estimate drifts further from the real position.
So method two corrects the position using satellite signals, just like a phone map.
What we usually call GPS.
And that's exactly what Russia attacks.
It blasts strong noise to drown the signal out, or sends fake signals to trick the drone into thinking it's somewhere else.
To fight back, the drones use a special antenna made of several antennas working together.
It blocks only the direction the jamming comes from, while still listening to the real satellites.
And if the satellite signal is lost completely, some drones reportedly look down with a camera and compare the ground with a stored map.
In short: self-calculating inertial navigation, satellite signals, and a camera.
Each one covers the others' weaknesses.

Building a good drone is only half the job.
The other half is getting through Russia's air defences, among the densest in the world.
First, plan the route.
Through the gaps, away from radars and air-defence sites, and very low.
The lower you fly, the later radar notices.
Next, overwhelm them with numbers.
An air-defence unit can only engage so many targets at once.
Send drones from many directions at once, mix in cheap decoys, and some will get through.
And most important of all is the money.
The interceptor missile that shoots down one drone can cost several times more than the drone itself.
Even when the defender wins every time, it spends more than the attacker.
And every air-defence system guarding Moscow is one less guarding the front.
