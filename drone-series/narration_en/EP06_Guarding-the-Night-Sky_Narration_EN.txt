The drone war is a contest of spear and shield.
So far, we have looked at Ukraine's attack drones.
From here on, we look at how Ukraine defends against drones.

It is the night of October 18th, 2026, in Ukraine.
Air-raid sirens sound across the cities.
People head down into metro stations and basements.
At that moment, hundreds of attack drones are flying in from Russia.
Ukraine's General Staff said Russia sent 463 attack drones that day.
And it announced that 418 of them were stopped.
That is nine out of every ten.
Behind that number are the people who guarded the sky all night.
Today, let's look at who guards Ukraine's sky on a night when drones pour in, and how they do it.

First, we need to know the opponent.
Most of the incoming drones are what Russia calls the Geran, a one-way attack drone.
It began as an Iranian design called the Shahed.
Russia brought it in, then started building and modifying it in its own factories in huge numbers.
It is a small uncrewed aircraft with a triangular wing and a rear propeller that flies itself to set coordinates and crashes into them.
Its engine sounds like a moped, so Ukrainians know what it is from the sound alone.
But not everything that flies in is a real attack drone.
Russia mixes in cheap decoys such as the Gerbera.
The aim is to confuse air defences and make them waste precious missiles.
There are reports that some decoys now carry explosives too.
So the defenders have to deal with all of them, with no time to tell real from fake.
The drones usually take off from several places after dark and come in from many directions over several hours.
As we saw in Episode 1, the idea is to swarm in at once and wear the air defences out.

This is where the defenders' dilemma begins.
One Geran is estimated to cost tens of thousands of dollars.
A decoy costs about 10,000 dollars, by Ukrainian intelligence estimates.
A single Patriot interceptor missile, by contrast, costs several million dollars.
Stopping tens-of-thousands-dollar drones with multi-million-dollar missiles means losing more with every one you stop.
Let's do the simple math.
Stopping all 463 drones from that day with Patriot missiles would cost over 1.8 billion dollars in missiles in a single night.
That is more than 2 trillion Korean won.
No country could keep that up for weeks of nights like this.
And missiles have to be saved for deadlier threats such as ballistic missiles.
So Ukraine's rule is simple.
Cheap drones get stopped with cheap weapons.

The first people to show that rule in action were the mobile fire teams.
They mount machine guns or anti-aircraft guns on the back of pickups or old army trucks.
Then they go out ahead of time and wait where the drones are expected to pass.
To avoid being spotted, they reportedly switch off their engines and lights and hide in the dark.
When the alert comes in, they sweep the sky with thermal cameras and searchlights.
As the sound gets closer, a searchlight catches the drone and the gunner pulls the trigger.
It is a line of defence built from people, trucks and guns, without expensive equipment.
But the limits are clear.
Machine guns can barely reach anything above about 2 kilometres.
So Russia began flying its drones higher and faster to avoid the fire teams.
And if a drone shifts its route by just a few kilometres, the ambush comes up empty.

The next layer is anti-aircraft guns.
The Gepard anti-aircraft tanks sent by Germany find targets with radar and fire twin cannons.
They are known to be especially reliable against low-flying drones.
Shoulder-fired anti-aircraft missiles are also used against drones.
Then come the people in the sky.
They are the F-16 and Mirage pilots of Ukraine's Air Force.
The pilots take off in the middle of the night and hunt drones.
In May 2025, a photo was released of an F-16 returning with only one missile left.
The Air Force explained that the other five missiles had all hit their targets.
When the missiles run out, pilots sometimes close in and fire at drones with the cannon.
But getting close to an exploding drone is dangerous for the pilot too.

Now there is a new hunter in the sky.
It is the drone that catches drones, the interceptor drone.
From the ground, a pilot watches a screen and flies a small, fast drone into a Geran.
An interceptor drone is reported to cost about 1,000 to 2,000 dollars.
That is a few thousandths of a missile's price.
Germany agreed to pay for 15,000 interceptor drones for Ukraine's National Guard.
One report said that in January 2026, interceptor drones accounted for about 70 percent of the Shahed-type drones Ukraine shot down.
But this figure comes second-hand from one media report and is not an officially confirmed number.
The FPV piloting skills we saw in Episode 3 are now being used to guard the sky.

All of these people move together like a single net.
Radar and observation posts report each drone's position and heading.
Command posts take that information and decide which layer handles which drone.
Broadly, fighters take the drones coming in from far away, and guns and fire teams take the ones near the cities.
Interceptor drones fill the gaps in between.
If a drone gets through one layer, the next one is waiting.

But stopping 418 also means 45 were not stopped.
After nights like this, cities count the damage.
Burned apartments, power cuts, shattered windows.
Ukrainian media pointed out that even a very high interception rate does not guarantee complete safety.
Every single drone that gets through threatens someone's home and life.
And Russia keeps changing its drones to break through the defences.
The biggest change is speed.
New jet-powered Gerans fly in two to three times faster than the older drones.
On October 17th, President Zelensky said only about 55 to 60 percent of the jet-powered drones are being shot down.
That speed is hard to catch for machine guns and propeller interceptor drones alike.

Guarding the night sky is not about one high-tech weapon but about many people stacking defences layer upon layer.
But when the attacker gets faster, the defence has to get faster too.
